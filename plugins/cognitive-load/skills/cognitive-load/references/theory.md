# Cognitive load theory, as far as it was read

What the theory says, what it no longer says, and where it stops applying
to code. `sources.md` says where each statement was read. Most primary
papers were not reachable whole, and each quotation below says whether it
comes from a paper's abstract, from a full text, or from a secondary
source.

## Origin

John Sweller, "Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on
Learning", *Cognitive Science* 12(2), 1988. Its abstract, read from a
public mirror: conventional problem solving "in the form of means-ends
analysis requires a relatively large amount of cognitive processing
capacity which is consequently unavailable for schema acquisition". The
abstract does not contain the words intrinsic, extraneous or germane. The
three kinds of load came later.

- **Intrinsic** and **extraneous** load: Wikipedia's article attributes
  both terms to Chandler and Sweller, "Cognitive Load Theory and the
  Format of Instruction", *Cognition and Instruction* 8(4), 1991. Its
  definitions there: intrinsic is "the inherent level of difficulty
  associated with a specific instructional topic"; extraneous "is
  generated by the manner in which information is presented to learners
  and is under the control of instructional designers". The 1991 paper
  was not opened.
- **Germane** load and the model in which the three add up: Sweller, van
  Merriënboer and Paas, "Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design",
  *Educational Psychology Review* 10, 1998. Its abstract, read from a
  mirror, describes "a limited capacity working memory" and "an
  effectively unlimited long-term memory, holding schemas that vary in
  their degree of automation". The body was not opened, and the additive
  formula is attributed to it from memory only.

## What changed

Sweller, "Element Interactivity and Intrinsic, Extraneous, and Germane
Cognitive Load", *Educational Psychology Review* 22, 2010. Its abstract,
read from a mirror: "germane cognitive load can be defined in terms of
intrinsic cognitive load, thus also associating germane cognitive load
with element interactivity." Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas, "Cognitive
Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later", *Educational
Psychology Review* 31, 2019: its abstract was read from a mirror; one
sentence of its body was read only as a third party quoted it, that the
authors now hold germane load to "redistribute working memory resources
from extraneous activities to activities directly relevant to learning".
Kalyuga, "Cognitive Load Theory: How Many Types of Load Does It Really
Need?", *Educational Psychology Review* 23, 2011, argues the third type is
redundant. It was not opened, and that summary is a search engine's.
Wikipedia's article says the same in its lead: "the additivity of these
types of cognitive load has been investigated and questioned."

So this skill works with two loads, intrinsic and extraneous, and treats
"germane" as a name for what freed working memory is used for. It gives
nobody an instruction to protect germane load. No source read here gives
one.

## Element interactivity

The 2010 abstract calls element interactivity "the basic, defining
mechanism of intrinsic cognitive load": how many elements a task requires
a person to process at the same time, relative to what they already hold
as one schema. It is a construct, not an effect. It is also the quantity
this skill asks the user to count.

## Capacity

Cowan, "The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of
mental storage capacity", *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 24, 2001. Its
abstract, read from a mirror: Miller's seven "was meant more as a rough
estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit"; the
limit is observed "when other steps are taken specifically to block the
recoding of stimulus items into larger chunks"; "A single, central
capacity limit averaging about four chunks is implicated".

Miller, "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two", *Psychological
Review* 63, 1956, read whole from a public mirror. On chunking: "Since the
memory span is a fixed number of chunks, we can increase the number of
bits of information that it contains simply by building larger and larger
chunks, each chunk containing more information than before." On the
number: "I suspect that it is only a pernicious, Pythagorean coincidence."

What this means for code. The limit is on unfamiliar elements. A reader
who holds a pattern as one schema pays one chunk for it. The same code
costs a newcomer several. That is why the author of a piece of code is the
wrong judge of its load, and why the skill measures with newcomers.

## The effects

Cognitive load theory's findings are stated as effects: a change in
instructional material that changed learning. Wikipedia's article names
those demonstrated in the 1990s: "the completion-problem effect; modality
effect; split-attention effect; worked-example effect; and expertise
reversal effect". The catalogue is larger. A search engine's summary of
Sweller, Ayres and Kalyuga's 2011 book lists about fifteen, among them
goal-free, isolated elements, variability, redundancy, self-explanation,
imagination and transient information. The book was not opened. Any list
of ten is a selection.

Three were read in their Wikipedia articles:

- **Worked-example effect.** "Improved learning observed when worked
  examples are used as part of instruction, compared to other
  instructional techniques such as problem-solving." For code: a solved example beside the API
  beats a reference alone, for a reader who has not used it.
- **Split-attention effect.** "It is apparent when the same modality (e.g.
  visual) is used for various types of information within the same
  display. Users must split their attention between the materials, for
  example, an image and text, to understand the information being
  conveyed." For code: the fact a reader needs sits in another file, a
  wiki page or a configuration, and must be held while reading this one.
  Text plus a separate diagram is this effect, not the modality effect.
- **Expertise reversal effect.** "The reversal of the effectiveness of
  instructional techniques on learners with differing levels of prior
  knowledge." Techniques that help novices "are more effective for novices
  or low-knowledge individuals"; for experts "reduced guidance often
  results in better performance than well-guided instruction". For code:
  a comment that walks a newcomer through a loop is noise to the person
  who wrote it, and both readers exist.

Every effect was established on instructional material for learners. Its
application to a code base is an analogy until a study on code shows it.
`measures.md` carries the studies on code that were read.

## Where the theory stops

Cognitive load theory is a theory of learning. A developer reading code is
not always learning it. They are often trying to complete a task with the
least understanding that suffices. The practitioner text this skill draws
on says so plainly: "We are going to use 'cognitive load' in an informal
sense; sometimes it lines up with the specific scientific concept of
Cognitive Load, but we don't know enough about where it does and doesn't
match" (`patterns.md`, and `sources.md` for the copy). This skill keeps
that distinction. Where it says "load", it means what a reader must hold
to do a task, and it says which sense it is in when the difference
matters.
